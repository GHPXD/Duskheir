# Motivation, Choice, and Challenge

Use this reference to define why the intended audience may engage, what they repeatedly decide, and how the game pushes back.

## Motivation as a Design Hypothesis

Motivation describes what value a player may seek from play. It does not predict a person's behavior in every game. A player can pursue different values from moment to moment.

Common hypotheses include:

- **Mastery:** Improve execution, planning, or system knowledge.
- **Discovery:** Reveal spaces, rules, combinations, stories, or surprises.
- **Expression:** Make a build, performance, space, character, or solution feel personal.
- **Completion:** Finish bounded goals, sets, records, or long-term projects.
- **Connection:** Cooperate, compete, trade, teach, witness, or belong.
- **Narrative curiosity:** Learn what happened, what follows, or how a character changes.
- **Relief:** Enter a predictable rhythm, low-pressure routine, or restorative space.

Convert each hypothesis into observable evidence. For example, if mastery is central, players should retry because they can identify a better action, not only because a reward meter is nearly full.

## Anatomy of a Meaningful Choice

A choice becomes meaningful when the player can form an intention, distinguish alternatives, accept a tradeoff, and observe a consequence.

Audit five parts:

1. **Legibility:** Does the player know what can be chosen?
2. **Viability:** Can more than one option reasonably advance a goal?
3. **Tradeoff:** What does each option gain and give up now or later?
4. **Expression:** Can the decision reveal a preference, plan, or judgment?
5. **Response:** Does the game acknowledge the result soon enough to teach?

Use these dimensions to vary decisions without inventing arbitrary categories:

- Information: complete, uncertain, or intentionally hidden.
- Urgency: reflective, timed, or reactive.
- Reversibility: freely reversible, costly to undo, or permanent.
- Horizon: immediate, encounter-long, run-long, or persistent.
- Value tension: safety versus speed, flexibility versus efficiency, self-interest versus group benefit, certainty versus upside.

### Common Failure Modes

- **Decorative choice:** Options lead to the same relevant state. Keep it only if expression is the explicit value.
- **Solved choice:** One option dominates across expected contexts. Add a situational weakness, opportunity cost, or different goal.
- **Blind choice:** The consequence cannot be reasonably anticipated. Add clues, a preview, a low-cost trial, or frame it honestly as a gamble.
- **Choice overload:** The player cannot compare the options. Group them, defer some, or provide a baseline recommendation.
- **False permanence:** Presentation implies commitment but reload or respec rules trivialize it. Align the message and actual rule.
- **Punitive permanence:** A choice made before the player understands the system damages hours of progress. Offer reversible early experiments or a recovery route.

## Designing Challenge

Challenge is the relationship between a demand and the player's current ability to answer it. Difficulty is only one property of that relationship.

Possible demands include:

- Perception: notice a cue or relevant state.
- Comprehension: infer or remember a rule.
- Planning: compare future states and allocate resources.
- Execution: aim, time, steer, or coordinate inputs.
- Adaptation: revise a plan after uncertainty resolves.
- Endurance: sustain attention or performance over time.
- Social coordination: communicate roles, timing, or shared intent.

Name the demand before changing a number. Doubling enemy health increases duration and error exposure; it may not create a new tactical challenge.

### Challenge Ladder

For each skill or rule, sequence encounters through:

1. Safe observation.
2. Low-cost isolated use.
3. Required use with generous timing.
4. Variation in one condition.
5. Combination with a previously learned demand.
6. Strategic use where the player chooses when or whether to apply it.

Do not require mastery in the same moment a rule is introduced. A deliberately harsh opening can work when attempts are extremely short, feedback is immediate, and restart friction is nearly zero, but test that assumption with the intended audience.

## Risk, Reward, and Randomness

Risk needs a readable downside, a plausible probability or uncertainty model, and a reward that matters to the current decision. More reward does not repair an unknowable or unavoidable loss.

Check:

- Can the player estimate the danger from prior evidence?
- Can skill, preparation, or retreat change the outcome?
- Is the reward useful now, or merely numerically larger?
- Does failure remove time already spent without teaching anything?
- Can a low-probability event decide a high-stakes persistent outcome?
- Are paid random outcomes involved? If so, require transparent odds, legal review, spending safeguards, and an ethical review.

Use seeded randomness for repeatable tests. Test both average outcomes and streaks. A 10 percent success chance can still fail dozens of times; design pity rules or alternate acquisition paths when long streaks would violate the experience target.

## Verification

### Unit and Simulation Tests

- Assert that every listed option is reachable under its documented preconditions.
- Search small state spaces for a choice that dominates every alternative.
- Simulate random outcomes across many seeds and inspect tails, not only averages.
- Verify that challenge parameters remain within valid ranges and never create impossible timing windows.
- Verify recovery rules after failure, cancellation, disconnect, and reload.

### Visual and Play Tests

- Hide designer commentary and ask players to predict outcomes before choosing.
- Ask players to describe why they failed and what they will try next.
- Plot attempts, completion times, damage taken, unused actions, and quit points.
- Test cues without color, without audio, and under visual clutter.
- Compare first-time, returning, expert, and accessibility-aided play.

The design is ready to expand when players make different defensible choices, understand the game's response, and can improve without being told the solution.
